# Let the map decide the mode

The strongest finding across all six questions is that **map context beats sensor cleverness**. For travel modes, speed and motion sensors reliably split walking, cycling and "motorised", but no published method separates bus, car, tram, metro and train well from speed alone. What closes that gap is matching the trip against the transport network: stations, stops and track geometry. The same OpenStreetMap route relations that LifePath needs for snapping trips to rails are therefore also its best mode classifier. The owner's two proposed signals both hold up, but in narrower roles than proposed. Top speed is the weakest single feature in the classic Geolife study, so it should be replaced by a high percentile (P95) and used as a coarse gate. GPS signal loss is real and physically explained by metallised train glazing, but no study has measured it on iPhones, so it should be a soft hint that only counts when a station sits at both ends of the gap. For stops, the proven pattern is Arc's: never let iOS pause the session, decide "stopped" after about 2 minutes, then check briefly every 6 to 30 seconds, so a traffic light or platform wait costs seconds of track at most. For drawing, the one-overlay-one-custom-renderer design on MKMapView is Apple's own advice. MKTileOverlay is the source of most flicker reports from iOS 15 to iOS 18, and animating a renderer's alpha caused a 38-second stall on iOS 18. For lines, Visvalingam-Whyatt with a stored, monotone "importance" per point gives nested point sets, so a line only gains or loses points as you zoom and never changes shape. For iOS 26 design, Apple's guidance is a full-bleed map, one inset glass sheet, one small group of glass buttons, system components only, and a muted map style so LifePath's own layers stand out. Most of the exact thresholds below are design starting points to tune on the owner's own days, not published values. That is the main open risk throughout.

## Speed and motion split the easy modes; stations and track split the rest

### Recommended approach

Classify in two layers. First, cut each trip into single-mode pieces at walking stretches. Then classify each piece with cheap features, and let route geometry decide between the motorised modes. Geolife's segmentation is the reference method. A point counts as a "possible walk point" when speed is below **1.8 m/s (6.5 km/h)** and acceleration below **0.6 m/s²**, and the start and end of each walk segment are the candidate mode changes, because people stop and walk when they change mode ([Zheng et al. WWW 2008](https://archives.iw3c2.org/www2008/papers/pdf/p247-zhengA.pdf)). The same work found that **car, bus and bike are followed by walking 95 to 98% of the time** ([Zheng et al. WWW 2008](https://archives.iw3c2.org/www2008/papers/pdf/p247-zhengA.pdf)), which justifies a small smoothing step that forbids implausible sequences such as car directly into bus.

For each piece, the features with real evidence behind them are traffic-robust ratios rather than raw speed. **Stop rate** counts points below 3.4 m/s per unit distance. **Heading change rate** counts turns above 19° per unit distance. **Velocity change rate** counts speed jumps above 26% per unit distance. Combined with a few speed values, these reached **72.8% accuracy by distance on walk, bike, bus and car**, and graph post-processing added about four points ([Zheng et al. UbiComp 2008](https://microsoft.com/en-us/research/wp-content/uploads/2016/02/Ubicomp270-yuzheng.pdf)). A 2024 location-only study on eight classes, including train and subway, used one-minute windows of speed mean and spread, acceleration mean and spread, and "movability" (straight-line distance divided by path length). It reached **88.1% accuracy, rising to 92.6% with a hidden Markov model (HMM) smoothing layer** ([arXiv 2404.15323](https://arxiv.org/html/2404.15323v1)). The SHL challenge's 2018 winner also used HMM smoothing ([Wang et al. 2021](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/computer-science/articles/10.3389/fcomp.2021.713719/full)). Two independent results therefore agree that a small smoothing step is worth about 4 to 5 points. CoreMotion's activity classes (stationary, walking, running, cycling, automotive) come from the motion coprocessor and cost almost nothing, so they make a good first-pass prior. LocoKit, the engine behind Arc, feeds its classifier exactly this sensor set: filtered speed, course variance, accuracy, pedometer cadence, CoreMotion activity and device acceleration ([LocoKit ActivityBrainSample.swift](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/sobri909/LocoKit/master/LocoKit/Base/ActivityBrainSample.swift)).

The motorised split then comes from the map. Adding GIS data (bus stops, rail lines) to GPS lifted accuracy to **over 93.5%, 17 points above GPS alone**, in Stenneth et al.; that study also used live bus positions, which a private offline app cannot have ([Stenneth et al. 2011](https://typeset.io/pdf/transportation-mode-detection-using-mobile-phones-and-gis-2o9lv4ngro.pdf)). A trip that fits a rail relation between two stations, with a smooth speed profile and near-zero heading change, is rail. A road trip that dwells at mapped bus stops and fits a bus relation is bus. Anything else on roads is car. When no option clearly wins, LifePath should store "motorised" rather than guess. LocoKit's documentation recommends exactly this fallback to a generic class when the best match scores low ([ActivityTypeClassifier.swift](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/sobri909/LocoKit/master/LocoKit/Timelines/ActivityTypes/ActivityTypeClassifier.swift)).

### The owner's top-speed proposal: right idea, wrong statistic

Speed does carry information, but **top speed is among the weakest single features measured**. In Geolife, the top velocity alone scored 0.257 accuracy by distance and top acceleration 0.208, against 0.561 for stop rate or heading change rate. The authors' explanation is that top speed depends on traffic ([Zheng et al. 2008](https://microsoft.com/en-us/research/wp-content/uploads/2016/02/Ubicomp270-yuzheng.pdf)). A single GPS spike also sets the maximum. Huss et al. found the **95th-percentile speed** the most useful speed metric, but even combined with acceleration it only separated "motorised" reliably, with train, bus and car pooled into one class ([Huss et al. 2014](https://ij-healthgeographics.biomedcentral.com/articles/10.1186/1476-072X-13-40/tables/1)). The open-source Dawarich rules show the overlap plainly: bus 10–100 km/h, driving 15–220, train 30–350 with a minimum of 80. Dawarich's own documentation admits fast highway driving gets labelled train and most buses get labelled driving ([Dawarich docs](https://dawarich.app/docs/features/transportation-modes/)). The owner's idea survives as a gate: use P95 over the segment, not the maximum.

| Gate (starting values to tune on own data) | Basis |
|---|---|
| P95 below ~8 km/h → walk | Geolife walk threshold 6.5 km/h; Dawarich walking 1–7 km/h |
| ~8–35 km/h, low stop rate, CoreMotion cycling → bike | Dawarich cycling 7–45 km/h |
| P95 above ~35 km/h → motorised, then geometry decides | Huss: P95 only separates motorised |
| P95 above ~80 km/h with smooth profile → rail or motorway car, track match decides | Dawarich train minimum 80 km/h |

These cut-offs are a synthesis of the Dawarich and Geolife numbers, not a validated table. No study publishes per-mode P95 values.

### The owner's signal-loss proposal: physically sound, unmeasured on phones

The physics supports the idea that rail vehicles block GPS more than cars. Ordinary window glass costs about **1–4 dB** at the GPS L1 frequency, while metallised glazing costs around **20 dB** or more ([arXiv 2007.14329](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2007.14329)). A UK Department for Transport study of train carriages measured **3–28 dB** of loss, with window seats at 3–7 dB and metallised glazing adding 20–30 dB. It measured mobile-network frequencies, not GPS, though they sit in a similar range ([UK DfT](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/618d299cd3bf7f055b29332f/mobile-connectivity-in-rolling-stock-radio-frequency-attenuation-characteristics.pdf)). A receiver beside a high-speed-train window failed to track satellites on a real journey ([arXiv 2007.14329](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2007.14329)). Researchers also note that phones lose GPS underground, in stations, and away from vehicle windows ([Urbancic et al. 2016](https://aile3.ijs.si/dunja/SiKDD2016/Papers/Urbancic_TransportationMode.pdf)). A navigation patent infers train travel from signal loss and recovery at known tunnel and station locations ([US 8983766](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/8983766)).

The evidence also limits the proposal. iOS does not expose signal strength or satellite counts, so LifePath only sees missing fixes and worsening `horizontalAccuracy`. No study has used that as a classifier feature or measured it on an iPhone. A window seat on a train keeps fixes, and a phone in a car's footwell or a building lobby loses them. The proposal should therefore be implemented as a soft hint with a check attached. A gap counts toward metro or train when **both ends are near mapped stations or entrances** and the speed implied across the gap is plausible for rail. A gap with no station at either end is just tunnel or indoors. In the 2024 location-only study, subway was the class where location data carried the least weight (0.19 against 0.81 for acceleration) ([arXiv 2404.15323](https://arxiv.org/html/2404.15323v1)), so underground, the gap itself is the evidence.

### Open risks

No study separates tram from bus or metro from train on GPS speed alone, and train against subway is a persistent confusion even with motion sensors ([Wang et al. 2021](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/computer-science/articles/10.3389/fcomp.2021.713719/full)). All Geolife thresholds were tuned at 2-second sampling on four modes, not on iOS at 1 Hz. Boats and ferries had no source; matching against water areas and `route=ferry` relations is the obvious cue but untested. Background CoreMotion only delivers while a location session keeps the app awake. One iOS 26.5 report saw 99.5% of motion callbacks delivered with location running and 0.16% without ([Apple forum 841001](https://developer.apple.com/forums/thread/841001)).

## Route relations give rail its true shape when checked against the trace

### Recommended approach

Snapping is a two-stage problem: pick the line, then replace the noisy trace with that line's geometry between the boarding and alighting stops. OSM's PTv2 schema provides the data. Each `type=route` relation (`route=train|subway|light_rail|tram|bus|trolleybus|ferry|monorail`) lists its stops and platforms in order, followed by its ways from start to terminus. Each direction and variant is its own relation ([OSM wiki: Public transport](https://wiki.openstreetmap.org/wiki/Public_transport)). Because the stop order encodes direction, a candidate line must contain a boarding stop **before** an alighting stop. That rule resolves the opposite direction and most shared corridors.

The recipe that follows from the sources has five steps. Find candidate stations within roughly 150–300 m of the first and last good fixes. Keep relations whose member stops include both, in the right order. Project the trace onto each candidate's polyline and require steady forward progress and a small median distance (a starting value of about 50 m for rail and 30 m for bus). Then output the relation's geometry between the two stops, with the stops as waypoints, and keep the raw trace separately. When signal is lost underground, the stops on either side pin the path; the time across the gap is spread by distance along the track, never drawn as a straight line. The thresholds are design suggestions without a validating source.

This is a simplified form of published, tested methods. Bast and Brosi's pfaedle maps sequences of stations onto OSM rail and road graphs with an HMM. It models turn restrictions, penalises U-turns on open track, and charges extra cost for track not covered by a matching route relation. **Most paths came within 20 m of ground truth**, and for trains even simple baselines worked well because few stations compete. Tram and subway matching ran in milliseconds to seconds ([Bast & Brosi 2018](https://ad-publications.informatik.uni-freiburg.de/SIGSPATIAL_Sparse%20map%20matching%202018.pdf)). That speed fits a phone. pfaedle is GPL-3.0 ([pfaedle GitHub](https://github.com/ad-freiburg/pfaedle)), so LifePath should reimplement the idea rather than copy the code. When no relation fits, the fallback is a small Viterbi search (the standard HMM path-finding step) over raw `railway=rail|subway|light_rail|tram` ways near the trace, in the style of Newson and Krumm ([Microsoft Research](https://www.microsoft.com/research/publication/hidden-markov-map-matching-noise-sparseness/)). Unmatched bus trips keep their raw trace or snap to roads.

For Overpass, use two cached queries. First, an index per area that returns only tags and bounding boxes (`out tags bb`) for route relations and stations near the trip. Then fetch the full geometry (`out geom`) once per chosen relation ID and cache it permanently ([Overpass QL wiki](https://wiki.openstreetmap.org/wiki/Overpass_API/Overpass_QL)). The main public instance allows about **10,000 requests and under 1 GB per day**, returns 429 or 504 when busy, and its wiki asks regular application users to stay far below that ([Overpass API manual](https://dev.overpass-api.de/overpass-doc/en/preface/commons.html)). One person's diary sits far below that limit. LifePath still needs retry with backoff, because throttling is the practical failure. Only bounding boxes and relation IDs leave the phone, never the track. OSM-derived geometry must credit "© OpenStreetMap contributors" ([pfaedle GitHub](https://github.com/ad-freiburg/pfaedle)).

### Evidence on "worldwide"

Worldwide coverage is the weak point of this approach. Relation quality varies by place. The OSM wiki notes that stop positions are often missing or wrong because few maps render them ([OSM wiki: Public transport](https://wiki.openstreetmap.org/wiki/Public_transport)). Member order breaks after ways are split in forking or looping routes, and wrong roles and duplicated ways are common mistakes ([OSM wiki: Relation:route](https://wiki.openstreetmap.org/wiki/Relation:route)). Bus coverage is uneven even in large European cities: the Glasgow page says its bus relations "need a lot of work" ([OSM wiki Glasgow](https://wiki.openstreetmap.org/Glasgow/PublicTransport)). A parser must therefore treat member ways as an unordered set, sort them by connectivity, and use stops only as an ordered hint. GTFS route shapes are not a better worldwide source. `shapes.txt` is optional and missing from most feeds in one study ([Wu thesis 2022](https://ad-publications.informatik.uni-freiburg.de/theses/Bachelor_Robin_Wu_2022.pdf)), licences vary per feed ([Transitland](https://transit.land/documentation/datastore/feeds.html)), and whole-agency downloads reveal the region. GTFS can be an optional extra, never a dependency.

### Open risks

Two lines that share track and stops cannot be told apart without timetables. Live-trace matching drops in accuracy where trams and buses share streets or two trips serve the same stops at the same time ([Wu thesis 2022](https://ad-publications.informatik.uni-freiburg.de/theses/Bachelor_Robin_Wu_2022.pdf)). In that case LifePath should show the shared track and the set of possible lines, not pick one. No per-region completeness figures exist; rail, metro and tram relations are believed to be far more complete than bus, but that was not verified. Overpass response sizes for large cities were not measured, so a test query on Amsterdam, London or Tokyo should come before the cache design is finalised.

## Stops stay cheap when tracking never pauses

### Recommended approach

The owner's goal of no gaps on short waits is mainly about which iOS features to avoid. Apple's documentation states that once `pausesLocationUpdatesAutomatically` pauses updates, restarting them is the app's job, and with When-In-Use permission a pause ends location access until the app is next opened. Apple suggests turning the property off and lowering accuracy in the background instead ([Apple docs](https://developer.apple.com/tutorials/data/documentation/corelocation/cllocationmanager/pauseslocationupdatesautomatically.md)). With `CLLocationUpdate.liveUpdates()`, an Apple engineer advises never stopping updates during a stationary pause. An app terminated while stopped is never relaunched when the device moves again, and the older setup of GPS plus significant-location-change plus region monitoring recovers "clunky" with possible misses ([Apple forum 810433](https://developer.apple.com/forums/thread/810433)). Geofences and significant-location-change are too coarse to restart a trip: region updates arrive roughly every 1–5 minutes ([Apple forum 757363](https://developer.apple.com/forums/thread/757363)), and significant-location-change needs about 500 m of movement ([O'Reilly excerpt](https://www.oreilly.com/library/view/ios-components-and/9780133086898/ch02lev2sec5.html)). They are only safety nets for relaunching the app after it has been killed.

Within that, Arc's open-source LocoKit2 is the only design with published code and long field use. It keeps a 999 m, no-distance-filter keep-alive running all day. It declares a stop when the filtered position stays inside a **20–100 m circle for 2 minutes**, with the radius scaled to recent accuracy, and then switches the precise GPS off ([SleepModeDetector.swift](https://github.com/sobri909/LocoKit2/blob/main/Sources/LocoKit2/Samplers/SleepModeDetector.swift)). While stopped, it turns precise GPS back on for up to 30 seconds every **6 to 60 seconds**. The interval adapts: 6–30 s at unknown places, longer at places where it has learned you stay ([TimelineRecorder.swift](https://github.com/sobri909/LocoKit2/blob/main/Sources/LocoKit2/Managers/TimelineRecorder.swift)). A 90-second traffic light never reaches the 2-minute threshold. A 7-minute platform wait does, but each check runs at most 6–30 s apart, so leaving costs only seconds of track.

Two separate thresholds keep the diary honest. A **power stop** at about 2 minutes saves battery and is invisible to the owner. A **diary stop** (a place visit) needs a longer dwell before it appears in the timeline, so platforms, bus stops and crossings never become "places". The stay-point literature spans 50 s to 20 minutes and 20–200 m ([arXiv 1701.06276](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1701.06276)). The classic setting is 200 m and 20 minutes ([arXiv 1904.09357](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1904.09357)), which suits places but is far too slow for power. The Lachesis method adds a maximum gap between pings inside a stay ([NOMAD docs](https://nomad.readthedocs.io/en/latest/stop_detection.html)). A resume after a gap therefore extends the stay unless the position has moved beyond the roaming radius, so a missed fix never splits one visit into two. On departure, the start time can be backfilled from CoreMotion's activity and pedometer history, which iOS can return after the fact ([Apple CMMotionActivityManager](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/coremotion/cmmotionactivitymanager)). The first stretch from the stay centre to the first moving fix should be drawn as inferred.

### Where the evidence disagrees

The notes for this report suggest a 3–5 minute "settling" period at full GPS before any power saving, to keep traffic lights and platform waits out of sleep. Arc uses 2 minutes and relies on fast check-ins instead. Neither number has a published test behind it. Arc's has years of field use, so it is the better default. If the owner sees platform waits cut into the track, raising the power-stop threshold near known stations is the obvious adjustment.

### Open risks

No one has published Apple's wake-up latency for resuming from a stationary pause, for CLMonitor exits, or for CoreMotion on iOS 26. Older CoreMotion figures are 5–10 seconds, with an iOS 16.4 report of delays of several minutes on some devices ([WWDC14 612](https://nonstrict.eu/wwdcindex/wwdc2014/612/); [Apple forum 725416](https://developer.apple.com/forums/thread/725416)). The behaviour of the `stationary` flag is undocumented ([Apple forum 750531](https://developer.apple.com/forums/thread/750531)). An unanswered iOS 26.4.2 report says apps swiped away in the App Switcher are not relaunched by significant-location-change or region monitoring, which did relaunch them on iOS 18 ([Apple forum 826964](https://developer.apple.com/forums/thread/826964)). The owner should never swipe LifePath away, and the app should show the time of the last recorded point so a dead session is noticed.

## One custom renderer per layer keeps fog and heat smooth

### Recommended approach

Stay on UIKit's MKMapView, and draw fog and heat each as **one MKOverlay with one custom MKOverlayRenderer**, not one overlay per track. SwiftUI's Map has no custom renderer ([Medium: SwiftUI MapKit iOS 17](https://medium.com/@gerdcastan/swiftui-mapkit-ios-17-the-missing-features-4b08fa42ee9f)). Apple's developer support recommended the same pattern for a lagging path display. It advised `MKMultiPolyline` when segments share a style, warned against removing and re-adding overlays because the map must redisplay without them, and suggested a custom renderer that redraws only the changed region ([Apple forum 759309](https://developer.apple.com/forums/thread/759309)). Many separate overlays are the known slow path. One iOS 16 report saw hundreds of polygons take about a minute to draw instead of 3 seconds ([Apple forum 715655](https://developer.apple.com/forums/thread/715655)).

Apple's documentation for `draw(_:zoomScale:in:)` sets the rules for the renderer. Draw only inside the requested map rectangle. Work in map points. Use the zoom scale for stroke widths. Expect drawing on several background threads at once, so share no mutable state ([Apple: draw](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/mapkit/mkoverlayrenderer/draw(_:zoomScale:in:).md)). When new points arrive, invalidate only the affected rectangles with `setNeedsDisplay(_:zoomScale:)` or `setNeedsDisplay(_:)` ([Apple: setNeedsDisplay](https://developer.apple.com/tutorials/data/documentation/mapkit/mkoverlayrenderer/setneedsdisplay(_:zoomScale:).md)). Inside `draw`, query a simple grid or R-tree for segments touching the tile. Build one path per tile. Rasterise into an offscreen tile bitmap, cache it by tile and zoom, and redraw only tiles hit by new data. This tiling-and-caching design is an inference from the sources, not a measured recipe.

**Avoid MKTileOverlay for generated layers.** Developers have reported flicker with it on iOS 15, 17 and 18: the whole screen blanks for a frame on every zoom change even with cached local tiles, and overlays vanish when an annotation updates ([Apple forum 696780](https://developer.apple.com/forums/thread/696780)). The `canReplaceMapContent` setting forces a trade-off. Setting it on flickers; setting it off makes Apple's base map download and draw underneath, which wastes data and energy ([Apple forum 770925](https://developer.apple.com/forums/thread/770925)). A plain overlay renderer composites over the base map, so neither problem arises.

### Semi-transparent fog without flicker

The most important rule for semi-transparent fog came from a reported iOS 18 bug. **Setting a renderer's `alpha` on every region change ran a Metal command on the main thread, stalled a dozen small tiles for about 38 seconds, and caused redraw flashes.** The fix was to leave `renderer.alpha` alone and apply transparency with `context.setAlpha` inside `draw` ([Apple forum 770925](https://developer.apple.com/forums/thread/770925)). So the fog's transparency must live in the drawing itself: fill the tile with fog colour at a fixed alpha, then erase the travelled paths with a clear blend. The erase width should be the larger of a minimum on-screen width and a real-world radius, so streets stay visible when zoomed out. Soft edges come from a few strokes of decreasing width and alpha, or a blur on the tile's alpha channel with a margin so tile seams do not show. The vector alternative is one world polygon with every explored area cut out as a hole ([turf.mask](https://atlas.co/functions/turf/turf-mask/)). Building that from years of tracks is harder than erasing strokes per tile, so it is a worse fit here. These fog steps are design inferences; no source documents how Fog of World or similar apps render.

### Heatmap that follows lines

Strava's experience supports drawing **paths, not points**. Its 2017 rebuild switched to rasterised paths at twice the resolution ([FlowingData](https://flowingdata.com/2017/11/21/global-cycling-and-running-heatmap)). Strava also found normalisation has a large visual impact. Instead of one global maximum, it ranks each pixel within a tile and its neighbours, then blends the four nearest tiles' rankings to hide tile edges ([Strava: Building the Global Heatmap](https://medium.com/strava-engineering/the-global-heatmap-now-6x-hotter-23fc01d301de), read via summaries only). Mapbox's GPU heatmap adds blurred kernels into a half-float texture at **a quarter of the resolution on each side (16 times fewer pixels)**, then maps density through a colour ramp that starts transparent. It claims 60 fps with 400,000 points ([Mapbox blog](https://www.mapbox.com/blog/introducing-heatmaps-in-mapbox-gl-js)).

For LifePath, the inference is this. Stroke track segments additively into a low-resolution count buffer per tile, with a margin that overlaps neighbouring tiles. Blur once. Apply a log mapping whose scale is fixed per zoom level and computed once from the whole personal dataset. Colour through a 256-entry lookup table. One person's data is small enough to compute a true maximum per zoom, which avoids Strava's neighbour blending and the tile-edge seams that a per-tile maximum would cause.

### Open risks

There are no published battery, memory or frame-rate numbers for any of these approaches on iPhone, for MapKit or MapLibre. The flicker reports run to iOS 18; nothing was found confirming or ruling them out on iOS 26. MapLibre Native (Metal-based since 2024, with built-in heatmap and fill layers) is a viable free fallback ([MapLibre](https://maplibre.org/news/2024-01-19-metal-support-for-maplibre-native-ios-is-here/)). It would replace Apple's base map, though, and its heatmap is point-based, so it is not worth switching unless MapKit fails on device. Core Image blur and colour-map cost per tile was not researched. Measuring in Instruments on the iPhone 15 Pro is the only reliable check. Map drawing also must stop entirely in the background: one iOS 26 app was suspended repeatedly because each location update triggered a 150 ms map redraw ([Apple forum 844579](https://developer.apple.com/forums/thread/844579)).

## Stored importance values keep lines identical at every zoom

### Recommended approach

The requirement that a line never changes shape across zooms has a clean, published solution. **Visvalingam-Whyatt** removes the point whose triangle with its two neighbours has the smallest area, then repeats ([Visvalingam, Hull repository](https://hull-repository.worktribe.com/output/376330)). Bostock's refinement stores each point's "effective area" as the larger of its own triangle and the last removed area. That makes the values monotone, so filtering by any cut-off gives nested point sets. The renderer then drops points below a cut-off instead of re-simplifying, and removing **95% or more of points often keeps enough detail** ([Bostock](https://bost.ocks.org/mike/simplify/)). TopoJSON uses the same scheme, with a cut-off below one square pixel at the displayed scale ([TopoJSON CLI](https://github.com/topojson/topojson-1.x-api-reference/blob/master/Command-Line-Reference.md); [Command-Line Cartography](https://medium.com/@mbostock/command-line-cartography-part-3-1158e4c55a1e)).

The implementation that follows is short. Compute one importance value per point **once**, when a day or trip is closed, with ties broken by point index so the result is deterministic. Store it beside the point. Pick one cut-off per whole zoom level, about 1 px² at that level's metres-per-pixel, and keep everything at maximum zoom. Cache the filtered index list per chunk and level. Zooming in only adds points and zooming out only removes them, so nothing jumps. Store tracks as immutable chunks with forced-keep endpoints, so later data never changes old geometry. Compute the cut-off from metres per pixel, not from the data's extent: mapshaper's resolution estimate from data extent can over-simplify ([mapshaper guide](https://mapshaper.org/docs/guides/simplification.html)).

### Douglas-Peucker is the weaker choice here

Douglas-Peucker, the usual default, bounds the maximum deviation, which makes it a good storage compressor. Its hierarchy does not guarantee thresholds that change monotonically across scales ([van der Poorten, Zhou & Jones](https://mysite.cs.cf.ac.uk/C.B.Jones/articles/vanderPoortenGIScience02.pdf)), it can self-intersect ([arXiv cs/0209007](https://arxiv.org/pdf/cs/0209007)), and at strong simplification it produces visible spikes ([mapshaper guide](https://mapshaper.org/docs/guides/simplification.html)). Tile-based tools such as Tippecanoe simplify each zoom separately to tile resolution ([Tippecanoe](https://github.com/anandthakker/tippecanoe)), which is exactly what makes shapes shift between zooms. LifePath can still apply a few-metre Douglas-Peucker pass on storage, but the display level of detail should come from the Visvalingam importance values.

### Keeping detail

Detail is lost before simplification as much as during it. Light smoothing on raw points (speed-gated outlier removal or a forward-backward Kalman smoother) should run first, then importance is computed on the result. Heavy smoothing rounds off switchbacks. Curves drawn on screen should use Catmull-Rom splines, which pass through every kept point, not Chaikin smoothing, which cuts corners inward and multiplies the point count ([PostGIS ST_ChaikinSmoothing](https://atlas.co/functions/postgis/st-chaikin-smoothing/)). Raw points stay on disk untouched.

### Open risks

No source measures corner fidelity of Visvalingam against Douglas-Peucker on GPS tracks, or gives iOS performance numbers. Mapshaper's "weighted" Visvalingam variant reduces spikes, but Visvalingam herself notes it differs from the original method ([Hull repository](https://hull-repository.worktribe.com/OutputFile/376364)), so plain area with index tie-breaks is the safer deterministic default. Snapped transit legs should get importance values computed on the snapped geometry, or the rail shape could degrade at low zoom.

## The map is the content; glass is one thin layer on top

### Recommended approach

Apple's iOS 26 guidance for Liquid Glass sets the structure directly. Glass "forms a distinct functional layer for controls and navigation elements" over content. Use system bars, sheets and controls, which adopt glass automatically. "Reduce your use of custom backgrounds." Apply custom glass sparingly, never stacked, and group custom glass shapes in a `GlassEffectContainer` ([Apple: Adopting Liquid Glass](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/technologyoverviews/adopting-liquid-glass.md)). For LifePath this means a full-bleed map under the status bar and a floating system tab bar, which can shrink on scroll. Add **one inset bottom sheet** with a small partial detent, `.medium` and `.large`, carrying today's summary and progress. Half sheets are inset so the map shows around them and turn more opaque at full height. A custom `presentationBackground` disables the system glass, so the sheet should have none ([Apple: Adopting Liquid Glass](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/technologyoverviews/adopting-liquid-glass.md); [Nil Coalescing](https://nilcoalescing.com/blog/PresentingLiquidGlassSheetsInSwiftUI)). Floating controls should be at most two or three icon buttons in one group: layers and recenter. Each needs an accessibility label, and text and icons should not be mixed in one group.

The map guidelines add four specifics. Keep the map interactive, because non-interactive elements that cover it break expectations. Use the **muted map style when the app has information-rich content that should stand out**, which fits LifePath's fog, lit streets and heat. Place overlays "above roads", which keeps buildings and labels readable, rather than "above labels". Keep the Apple logo and legal link visible about 10 pt above the sheet's lowest resting position ([Apple HIG: Maps](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/maps.md)). Strava follows the one-button pattern: one map-layers icon opens a menu with heatmap choices ([BikeBiz](https://bikebiz.com/strava-expands-mapping-tools-with-night-and-weekly-heatmaps/)). LifePath's layer menu (lit streets, fog, heat, plain) fits the same model.

### Companion screens and motivation

Comparable apps point to what the timeline, stats and settings screens should show. CityStrides counts a street once every node is visited and computes the percentage by street count rather than length. Users find the rising total motivating, but complain that the rule is hidden and that OSM errors such as private or closed streets hold the percentage down ([CityStrides community](https://community.citystrides.com/t/newbie-question-what-counts-towards-the-completion/19842); [Running with Rock](https://runningwithrock.com/city-strides/)). LifePath's coverage figure should therefore state its rule and denominator in plain words, which also serves the owner's accuracy priority. Streaks can backfire: people who break one are more likely to quit, so broken streaks should not be highlighted ([INSEAD Knowledge](https://knowledge.insead.edu/marketing/consumer-streaks-are-motivating-key-keeping-them-alive)). Expected rewards undermine intrinsic motivation, while positive feedback strengthens it ([Rework, citing Deci, Koestner & Ryan 1999](https://resources.rework.com/libraries/edtech-growth/gamification-in-learning)). Fog of World's chief complaint is data lost when changing phones ([AppFollow reviews](https://apps.appfollow.io/ios/fog-of-world/505367096?country=us)), so export and backup belong in plain sight in settings. Quiet, own-progress framing fits a single-user diary: new streets today, coverage percentage, personal bests. Skip streak counters, badge floods and per-milestone notifications.

For permissions, Apple names a diary app as the legitimate case for Always access. An app may request Always **only once**, after When In Use has been granted, and must still work if the user declines ([Apple: Choosing the Location Services Authorization](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/bundleresources/choosing-the-location-services-authorization-to-request.md)). The best moment for that single request is after the first lit street appears, with a screen explaining that data stays on the phone.

### Open risks

Legibility of glass over busy content such as maps is the most frequent Liquid Glass criticism. Reduce Transparency and Increase Contrast remove most of the translucency ([MacRumors](https://www.macrumors.com/2025/09/17/ios-26-liquid-glass-critiques/)), so each glass element and each fog colour should be checked with both settings on. Fog against explored areas must differ in brightness, not only hue, for colour-blind viewers. A purely visual map also needs a text equivalent in the sheet for VoiceOver, and map rotors can step through places ([WWDC20 10116](https://developer.apple.com/wwdc20/10116)). Apple's sheet, tab bar and materials HIG pages could not be retrieved, and no first-party breakdown of Apple Maps or Find My detent behaviour was found. Sources on generic "AI-looking" design (purple gradients, rows of rounded icon cards, decorative glass) are opinion blogs ([Noqta](https://noqta.tn/en/blog/ai-design-slop-overused-ui-patterns-fix-2026)). They still make a useful checklist against default-looking screens.

## Conclusion

The six questions share one answer. LifePath gets accuracy by deciding things once, from the right evidence, and storing the result. A transit leg should be classified and snapped once against OSM relations, with mode and line kept as data. A line's importance values should be computed once when a day closes, so the map never re-decides its shape. A fog or heat tile should be drawn once and redrawn only when new points touch it. A stop should be decided by LifePath's own rule, not by an iOS pause that may never resume. The owner's proposals fit this pattern once reframed: top speed becomes a P95 gate, and signal loss becomes evidence only when stations sit at both ends of the gap. Both rely on map context, and that is the main design implication: the Overpass index of stations and route relations is shared infrastructure for mode detection, snapping and the honest display of transit, not a side feature of the map.

What remains unknown is mostly on-device behaviour that no source has measured: how often an iPhone 15 Pro loses fixes inside a tram compared with a car, how fast iOS 26 resumes after a stationary pause, how much a tiled fog renderer costs in battery, and how complete bus relations are outside Western Europe. Each can be answered cheaply from the owner's own labelled days and Instruments traces. Every threshold in this report should be stored as a named, tunable setting, so that a week of real data can correct it without code changes.
