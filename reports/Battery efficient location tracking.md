# Sleep the GPS, wake it fast

All-day location apps keep battery use low by keeping the GPS off whenever you are not moving, and most of any day is spent not moving. Strava doesn't really solve all-day tracking. It runs the GPS at full accuracy, but only between a Start and a Stop you press, for one to five hours, and cleans the track up afterwards. Arc Timeline, the best all-day diary, does it differently. It keeps a cheap, low-accuracy "keep-alive" running all day so that iOS never puts the app to sleep. It switches to full GPS only while you move. While you are stopped, it switches the GPS back on for up to 30 seconds every 6 to 60 seconds to check whether you have left. Its developer says it can record for 24 to 48 hours on one charge. Your earlier app probably drained the battery for the usual reasons. It ran GPS-level accuracy all the time, including while you sat still. It woke the processor for every location fix and did real work each time, like drawing maps or saving to the database. It may also have mixed several location services in a way iOS penalises. The GPS chip itself is not the problem: it draws only a few milliwatts. What costs battery is everything the phone does around each fix. For your iPhone 15 Pro on iOS 26, the cheapest path is to reuse the open-source engine behind Arc (LocoKit2) or to copy its two-tier design. You also need to plan around two iOS 26 problems: apps you swipe away in the App Switcher no longer get relaunched, and the visit-detection service has a known bug. A realistic target is roughly **5–15% of the battery per day** on a mostly stationary day.

## The GPS chip is cheap; everything around it is expensive

The most useful thing to understand is where the battery actually goes. A modern dual-frequency GPS receiver of the kind in Pro iPhones is rated at about **4 milliwatts for the normal L1 band and about 6 milliwatts with the extra L5 band** ([Broadcom](https://investors.broadcom.com/news-releases/news-release-details/broadcom-introduces-worlds-lowest-power-l1l5-gnss-receiver)). Against an iPhone battery of roughly 13 watt-hours, that works out to about 0.05% per hour. That is our calculation: Apple doesn't say which GPS chip it uses or how much power it draws. Yet screen-off recording apps measurably use a few percent per hour. The difference comes from the main processor waking up for every fix, from the app's own code running each time, from the cellular and Wi-Fi radios, and above all from the screen and anything drawn on it.

Apple's guidance names two separate cost levers, and DIY apps usually get both wrong. The first is **accuracy**. "Requesting higher accuracy than you need causes Core Location to power up additional hardware," Apple warns. Core Location is Apple's location framework. The default accuracy setting is "best", so an app that never lowers it runs at maximum the whole time ([Apple Energy Efficiency Guide](https://developer.apple.com/library/archive/documentation/Performance/Conceptual/EnergyGuide-iOS/LocationBestPractices.html)). The second is **how often the app is woken and how much work it does each time**. A setting called `distanceFilter` tells iOS to deliver a new location only after you have moved a set number of metres. Many developers assume it saves battery, but reports say it sits above the GPS hardware. It cuts the number of times your app is woken, but the GPS stays on ([Stack Overflow archive](https://kaweah.freedombox.rocks/kiwix/content/stackoverflow.com_en_all_2023-11/questions/20446113/ios7-is-it-more-power-efficient-to-defer-updates-or-to-set-desired-accuracy-to); [mizutori](https://medium.com/@mizutori/tracking-highly-accurate-location-in-ios-vol-3-7cd827a84e4d)). Filtering out points in your own code ("only keep one fix per 100 metres") saves nothing at all, because iOS has already done the work of producing them ([Overland settings](https://overland.p3k.app/settings)).

Real-world failures follow the same pattern. One developer's app on iOS 26 used `kCLLocationAccuracyBestForNavigation`, the highest accuracy setting, meant for turn-by-turn driving directions. It had no distance filter and had iOS's automatic pause switched off. It also started two location services on the same location manager (the object an app uses to talk to Core Location). Every few seconds in the background it redrew a map with about 750 elements, which took about **150 milliseconds of processing per location update**. The redraw stopped the app from keeping up with its location updates, and iOS kept suspending it. The fix was to stop drawing while in the background, which brought each update down to under 20 milliseconds ([Apple forum 844579](https://developer.apple.com/forums/thread/844579)). Arc's developer makes the same point about the screen: the map view uses **as much battery in one minute as an hour or more of sleep mode** ([Big Paua forum 799](https://support.bigpaua.com/t/arc-timeline-recorder-still-drawing-quite-a-power-from-the-battery/799)). Gaia GPS, a hiking app, had a similar case: its battery drain turned out to come from the on-screen compass, not from recording ([Michael Tsai](https://mjtsai.com/blog/2023/05/02/reducing-gaia-gps-battery-drain)). The list of things a DIY tracker typically gets wrong is therefore short. It runs GPS-level accuracy all day, keeps the GPS on while you are still, does a lot of work on every update, keeps the screen or map busy, and combines location services carelessly. Apple warns that one of those services, significant-location-change, "can actually result in higher energy use if not employed effectively" ([Apple Energy Guide](https://developer.apple.com/library/archive/documentation/Performance/Conceptual/EnergyGuide-iOS/LocationBestPractices.html)).

## Strava spends battery freely because its sessions are short

Fitness apps don't publish their settings, and nobody has reverse-engineered them. Their second-by-second tracks and live pace displays imply full GPS accuracy, with `activityType = .fitness` (a hint that tells iOS what kind of movement to expect) and background location switched on for the whole session. Strava describes itself as GPS-only. It says Apple Maps and Google Maps "can seem accurate" because they also use cell towers, which can hide GPS problems ([Strava support](https://support.strava.com/cs/articles/15402040-reseni-potizi-s-gps-v-systemu-ios)). The battery cost is acceptable only because the session is short and you chose to start it. Strava's guidance says modern phones can record for more than five hours ([Strava support](https://support.strava.com/hc/en-us/articles/216917177-Garmin-and-Strava)). AllTrails quotes **10–15% per hour** for tracking apps as a rule of thumb ([AllTrails support](https://support.alltrails.com/hc/en-us/articles/360019244951-Saving-battery-while-using-Navigate-to-record-an-activity)). One test compared two identical iPhone 16s, one running a background location app. It found about **2% per hour extra while travelling** and **under one percentage point per day while sitting still**. The test ran once on one model, and the vendor tested its own product ([HeyPolo](https://heypolo.com/blog/how-does-location-sharing-affect-your-battery)). At a few percent per hour, the same settings left on for 16 waking hours would use most of a charge. No mainstream fitness app offers an always-on mode for that reason.

What fitness apps contribute to a diary is their clean-up work, not their recording settings. Strava's "auto-pause" was originally its own rule: it paused when no fix showed positive speed for more than 10 seconds ([Strava Engineering, 2014](https://medium.com/strava-engineering/improving-auto-pause-for-everyone-13f253c66f9e)). On its servers, Strava replaces GPS altitude with values from elevation databases ([Strava Elevation FAQ](https://support.strava.com/hc/en-us/articles/115001294564-Elevation-on-Strava-FAQs)). Apple's Workout app visibly smooths tracks and appears to drop points that don't fit the next one, which rounds off sharp corners ([the5krunner](https://the5krunner.com/2025/09/20/apple-watch-ultra-3-raw-data-accuracy-is-it-better-than-in-the-workout-app/)). Gaia filters out inaccurate points, smooths, then averages ([Gaia GPS help](https://gaiagpshelp.zendesk.com/hc/en-us/articles/216162057-Why-GPS-Track-Recording-Can-Be-Inaccurate)). Clean tracks are mostly the result of this processing after recording, not of better raw GPS. A private app can do all of it on the phone, with no server.

## The diary apps split into three strategies, and only Arc pairs high detail with quick wake-ups

All-day diaries solve a different problem: how to notice quickly that you have started moving without keeping the GPS on. The answers fall into three groups.

| Strategy | Who uses it | How it notices you've left | Path detail | Battery |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Wi-Fi/cell only, plus iOS visits | Life Cycle; OwnTracks "Significant"; Overland battery preset | Significant-location-change, roughly 500 m / 5 min | Places only, rough paths | Life Cycle claims **under 1% per day** ([App Store](https://apps.apple.com/gb/app/id1064955217)) |
| GPS while moving, geofence while stopped | transistorsoft library; Overland "Resume with Geofence" | iOS geofence exit, **about 200 m** even when set to 25 m ([transistorsoft](https://github.com/transistorsoft/react-native-background-geolocation/wiki/Philosophy-of-Operation)) | Good paths; first part of each trip is lost | Low; no published figures |
| Continuous GPS | OwnTracks "Move"; Overland "high resolution" | Never stops | Best | "A lot of battery" ([Overland](https://overland.p3k.app/settings)) |
| Duty-cycled GPS in an app iOS never suspends | Arc Timeline / LocoKit2 | Brief GPS check every **6–60 s** | Best | **24–48 h of recording per charge** (developer claim) ([forum 799](https://support.bigpaua.com/t/arc-timeline-recorder-still-drawing-quite-a-power-from-the-battery/799)) |

The first group uses significant-location-change (SLC), a cheap iOS service that uses cell towers and Wi-Fi to tell an app when you have moved roughly 500 metres. OwnTracks documents it as reporting at most once every five minutes ([OwnTracks booklet](https://owntracks.org/booklet/features/location/)). Life Cycle says it "does not use the GPS in the background", relying on cell and Wi-Fi only ([App Store](https://apps.apple.com/gb/app/id1064955217)). That is the floor for battery use, and it can't draw a walk. Wi-Fi positioning on older iPhones had a median error of about **74 metres**, and cell positioning about **600 metres** ([Zandbergen 2009](https://www.scribd.com/document/154262133/Zandbergen-TGIS-2009)).

The second group switches the GPS off and lets the app be suspended once you stop. It sets a geofence, a virtual circle iOS watches on the app's behalf, and iOS wakes the app when you leave the circle. The catch is that iOS needs **about 200 metres** of movement before it fires. That means a walk to the corner shop can go missing, and every trip starts a block late ([transistorsoft GeoConfig](https://transistorsoft.github.io/capacitor-background-geolocation/latest/interfaces/GeoConfig.html)). Arc's developer gives the same reason for rejecting geofences: they fire too late and suspend the app in the meantime ([forum 799](https://support.bigpaua.com/t/arc-timeline-recorder-still-drawing-quite-a-power-from-the-battery/799)).

### How Arc actually does it

Arc's open-source engine, LocoKit2, runs two location managers at once ([LocomotionManager.swift](https://github.com/sobri909/LocoKit2/blob/main/Sources/LocoKit2/Managers/LocomotionManager.swift)). The **recording manager** runs at roughly 10-metre accuracy (`kCLLocationAccuracyNearestTenMeters`) and only while you move. The **keep-alive manager** is set to 999-metre accuracy with no distance filter. It runs all the time and Arc throws away its locations. It exists only so that iOS keeps the app running. Arc decides you have stopped when your filtered position has stayed inside a small circle for **2 minutes**. The circle's radius is 20 to 100 metres, scaled to how accurate the recent fixes were ([SleepModeDetector.swift](https://github.com/sobri909/LocoKit2/blob/main/Sources/LocoKit2/Samplers/SleepModeDetector.swift)). Arc then turns the precise GPS off and goes to sleep. While asleep, a timer switches the precise GPS back on for up to 30 seconds. If the fix falls outside the circle, Arc starts recording again. Otherwise it goes straight back to sleep. "When Arc / LocoKit are in sleep mode there aren't any calculations," the developer writes ([forum 799](https://support.bigpaua.com/t/arc-timeline-recorder-still-drawing-quite-a-power-from-the-battery/799)).

Arc's cleverest feature is how often it checks. At a place it has learned, it estimates how likely you are to leave soon. When leaving is likely it checks every 6 seconds, and when it is very unlikely, every 60 seconds. With no learned place, the gap grows from 6 to 30 seconds over the first hour ([TimelineRecorder.swift](https://github.com/sobri909/LocoKit2/blob/main/Sources/LocoKit2/Managers/TimelineRecorder.swift)). So at home overnight, Arc might use the GPS for a few seconds each minute. Arc's field logs also record mistakes to avoid. An earlier rule for restarting the GPS after old fixes caused "~1,000 redundant full-accuracy spin-ups/day". The fix was to wait longer before restarting and to back off from 1 minute to 2, 5 and 10 minutes when the signal is bad. Indoor GPS drift can also wake the app falsely at night. Arc counters that by treating fixes at known places as less accurate than they claim, so the drift stays inside the circle ([LocomotionManager.swift](https://github.com/sobri909/LocoKit2/blob/main/Sources/LocoKit2/Managers/LocomotionManager.swift); [forum 518](https://support.bigpaua.com/t/can-i-pause-arc-overnight-to-save-battery/518)).

The real-world numbers are mixed. Users report Arc taking around **20–31% of the daily battery share** in iOS's Battery screen, against 2–5% for Google Maps. The developer points out that the Battery screen shows each app's share of the energy used that day, not a share of a full charge. A 50% share on a day that used half the battery is about 25% of a charge ([forum 799](https://support.bigpaua.com/t/arc-timeline-recorder-still-drawing-quite-a-power-from-the-battery/799)). On an old iPhone SE 2, running Arc overnight left about 68% by morning, against about 95% without it. Drift-induced false wakes were the likely cause ([forum 518](https://support.bigpaua.com/t/can-i-pause-arc-overnight-to-save-battery/518)). No one has published controlled, instrumented battery measurements for any of these apps, or any figures for iPhone 15 Pro on iOS 26.

## iOS 26 rewards Apple's newer location tools and penalises the old tricks

The ground has shifted since many tutorials were written, and several techniques you may have used before no longer work. **Deferred updates**, which batched GPS fixes so the app woke less often, are deprecated. The system has reported them as unavailable since iOS 10 ([Apple deprecated symbols](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/corelocation/deprecated-symbols); [Stack Overflow archive](https://kiwix.lema.org/stackoverflow.com_en_all_2023-11/questions/39498899/deferredlocationupdatesavailable-returns-no-in-ios-10)). Another old trick kept an app alive by turning accuracy and the distance filter all the way down while running significant-location-change at the same time. Since iOS 16.4 that combination gets apps suspended. An Apple engineer stated the rule for staying alive in the background: no distance filter, and accuracy of 100 metres or better ("less than 1000 meters" if given as a number). Alternatively, show the blue location indicator ([Apple forum 726945](https://developer.apple.com/forums/thread/726945)). Arc's 999-metre keep-alive follows that rule exactly.

Apple's newer location tools come in three parts. **`CLLocationUpdate.liveUpdates`** (iOS 17) delivers locations as a stream. It pauses automatically when you have been still for a while and resumes when you move, marking the pause with a `stationary` flag ([WWDC23 10180](https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2023/10180/)). **`CLBackgroundActivitySession`** (iOS 17) tells iOS that the app is legitimately tracking in the background. It always shows the blue indicator, and it must be started while the app is on screen. **`CLServiceSession`** (iOS 18) declares the level of permission the app needs. Since iOS 18, "Always" location permission only takes effect while the app holds one of these sessions ([WWDC24 10212](https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2024/10212/)). After a crash or a system shutdown, iOS can relaunch the app "in most cases". The app must then restart its sessions within seconds of launching. The automatic pause can't be switched off. One developer saw it start after **about 10 minutes** of stillness ([Apple forum 763696](https://developer.apple.com/forums/thread/763696)). Apple's support staff advise not stopping updates yourself, and warn that an app terminated during a pause "won't be launched again when the device starts moving" ([Apple forum 810433](https://developer.apple.com/forums/thread/810433)). The legacy setup does worse: an app that used only the old background-location switch was suspended within a minute or so of leaving the screen on iOS 26. An app holding a `CLBackgroundActivitySession` kept running indefinitely ([Apple forum 844579](https://developer.apple.com/forums/thread/844579)).

Two iOS 26 problems affect your design directly. First, **swiping the app away in the App Switcher now appears to stop all relaunching**. In an iOS 26.4.2 test, a force-quit app got no events from significant-location-change or a geofence during a 30-kilometre drive. The tester says iOS 18 did relaunch it in the same situation, and Apple hasn't replied ([Apple forum 826964](https://developer.apple.com/forums/thread/826964)). Second, **visit monitoring (`CLVisit`) is broken on some devices**. This is iOS's built-in "you arrived at / left a place" service. On some iPhones it stops delivering after updating to iOS 26.0 to 26.1, while ordinary location updates keep working, and only reinstalling fixes it. An Apple engineer called it a probable regression (FB21022960), and no fix has been confirmed ([Apple forum 807301](https://developer.apple.com/forums/thread/807301)). Core Motion, the framework that reads the motion chip (walking, driving, still, step counts), can't wake a suspended app either. It can, however, be asked afterwards what activity happened while the app was asleep ([Apple CMMotionActivityManager](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/coremotion/cmmotionactivitymanager)).

## A concrete blueprint for an always-on diary on iPhone 15 Pro

The simplest dependable route is **not to write the location engine yourself**. LocoKit2 is the engine behind Arc. It is open source under the MPL-2.0 licence, targets iOS 17 and later, and was last updated two days before this report ([LocoKit2](https://github.com/sobri909/LocoKit2)). It already contains the two-manager design, the 2-minute sleep rule, the adaptive check timer, Kalman filtering (a standard method for smoothing noisy positions), the stale-fix and drift handling, and a watchdog that restarts the wake-up timer if it silently stops. One caution: its current default keep-alive was introduced on 30 September 2026 and is labelled internally as an experiment. If you keep the blue indicator visible, which is fine for a private app, you can use the older, longer-tested setting instead (`useShieldRegime = false`, which uses `CLBackgroundActivitySession`). Apple says showing the indicator is enough on its own to avoid the iOS 16.4 suspensions ([Apple forum 726945](https://developer.apple.com/forums/thread/726945)).

If you build your own instead, the rules are as follows. **Use two tiers.** While you move, record at `kCLLocationAccuracyNearestTenMeters` (about 10 metres) with a small distance filter of a few metres. While you are still, turn the precise GPS off and keep the app alive with a 999-metre, no-distance-filter keep-alive. Hold a `CLBackgroundActivitySession` (or `CLServiceSession` with Always permission), and start it from the screen the first time. **Decide "stopped" quickly**, after about 2 minutes inside a 20–100 metre circle, rather than waiting for iOS's own pause, which takes 10–15 minutes ([Overland](https://overland.p3k.app/settings); [transistorsoft](https://transistorsoft.github.io/capacitor-background-geolocation/latest/interfaces/GeoConfig.html)). **Check in briefly while stopped**: up to 30 seconds of GPS every 30 seconds or so, and less often at places where you usually stay a long time. **Keep safety nets** for the system killing the app: significant-location-change and visit monitoring can relaunch it, and asking Core Motion for past activity after each wake fills in what was missed. Because of the iOS 26 visit bug, check visits against your own stop detection and warn yourself if visits stop arriving.

The list of things to avoid follows from the cost model. Never use `BestForNavigation`. Don't leave the default "best" accuracy on all day. Don't rely on `distanceFilter`, or on filtering in your own code, to save power. Don't add significant-location-change on top of low accuracy plus a distance filter. Draw no map, run no timers and update no screen while in the background. Make no network calls, which a private diary doesn't need anyway. Save points to the database in batches rather than on every fix, and keep logging light. These last two follow from the cost model; no one has measured them. Don't swipe the app away, because iOS 26 will then stop restarting it. Expect long journeys to cost more: Arc itself warns that more detail takes more energy ([Arc](https://www.bigpaua.com/arcapp/)).

For accuracy, do your clean-up on the phone. First, throw out bad fixes: any with a negative accuracy value (iOS's way of marking a position invalid), any older than about 10 seconds, any timestamped no later than the previous fix, and any that imply an impossible speed. transistorsoft rejects implied speeds over 60 metres per second and accuracy worse than 100 metres ([transistorsoft LocationFilter](https://transistorsoft.github.io/capacitor-background-geolocation/latest/interfaces/LocationFilter.html)). For drawing walks, a stricter cut-off of about 20–25 metres makes sense. Next, apply a light Kalman filter that uses each fix's reported accuracy as its uncertainty. LocoKit uses a movement allowance of 4 metres per second ([LocoKit KalmanFilter](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/sobri909/LocoKit/master/LocoKit/Base/KalmanFilter.swift); [ActivityBrain](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/sobri909/LocoKit/master/LocoKit/Base/ActivityBrain.swift)). While you are stopped, record no path points at all. Store the stop as a single visit, so you don't get scribbles from GPS drift. Use the phone's barometer (`CMAltimeter`) for height rather than GPS. For storage and display, simplify tracks with the Douglas–Peucker method at a tolerance of a few metres. It guarantees the simplified line never strays further than that from the original ([MDPI IJGI](https://mdpi.com/2220-9964/12/10/399/xml)). Don't snap walks to roads, because footpaths and parks would be wrong. The 15 Pro's dual-frequency GPS is a free bonus that iOS uses automatically, but real-world tests haven't shown a clear accuracy gain on phones ([Mapillary forum](https://forum.mapillary.com/t/have-iphones-with-dual-frequency-gps-improved-accuracy/10423)). Keep the outlier filter in place regardless.

## Conclusion

The main lesson is that battery life in an all-day tracker comes down to what the app does while you sit still. A diary is idle for perhaps 20 of every 24 hours, so even a strong GPS setting barely matters if it runs only while you move. What hurts is anything that keeps the processor, the screen or the GPS busy while you sit at a desk or sleep. That shifts the main design question from "what accuracy do I set?" to two others: how fast can you tell that you have stopped, and how cheaply can you check whether you have left? Arc's answer, a two-minute stop rule plus brief, adaptive GPS checks, is the only proven design that gives good paths without the 200–500 metre blind spot of geofences and significant-location-change.

The second lesson is that iOS itself is the moving target. Rules changed in iOS 16.4, iOS 18 and again in iOS 26. Arc's own developer changed the keep-alive method in September 2026. For a one-person private project, reusing LocoKit2 is probably worth more than any setting in this report, because it brings years of field fixes that no tutorial documents. Several numbers here rest on developer claims or one-off tests. The most valuable thing you can do after building it is measure it yourself: run it for a week, log battery level and wake-ups, and compare against the 5–15% per day target.
