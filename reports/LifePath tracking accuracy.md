# Test LifePath against realistic phone errors first

The biggest accuracy gain open to LifePath costs no battery at all. Make the automatic tests simulate errors the way phones produce them, then let the filter use two numbers the phone already sends and LifePath throws away. Field measurements on Android phones show that location errors drift slowly, staying correlated for roughly 25 to 65 seconds on top of a per-trip offset of 2 to 6 m, while LifePath's synthetic tests add fresh, independent noise to every fix. Its current bars (median 8 m live, 6 m smoothed) are therefore easier to pass in the test than on the street, and they reward averaging, which is the one trick drifting errors defeat. After that come small, battery-free changes in ranked order: per-fix speed and course accuracy as filter inputs, a "standing still" signal at stops from the motion chip LifePath already reads, two extra drift states in the offline smoother, and map-matching settings that suit a walker sampled every 7 seconds instead of a car sampled every second. Two questions need an experiment on the phone before LifePath acts on them, because Apple's own engineers say the `.fitness` activity type bends walks onto known paths before LifePath sees them, and that two location managers in one app can override each other's settings. The walk from the front door to the first fix can be drawn, but only as a dashed guess chosen with the pedometer's distance, never as recorded track. Heavier machinery (factor graphs, full step-and-compass tracking, an RTK receiver, BestForNavigation all day) fails at least one goal, mostly because it costs battery or money and nobody has shown a gain on the kind of input iOS gives an app. Much of what LifePath already does, such as the 65 m rough-fix cutoff, Best instead of BestForNavigation, the 200 m geofence and a map-matching sigma of max(4 m, hAcc), lines up with Apple's statements and with established map-matching engines.

## Nine changes, ranked by what they buy for the effort

The ranking weighs expected accuracy gain against code size and against the three equal priorities: accuracy, battery and a cool phone. Every change in the top seven adds either zero work on the phone or a few extra multiplications per fix, which is my estimate and has not been profiled. Evidence strength is rated as measured (someone measured the effect, usually on other phones), documented (Apple states the behaviour but nobody measured it) or reasoned (my inference, unmeasured anywhere).

## Phone errors drift for up to a minute, and the test suite assumes they don't

A Kalman filter (a running estimate that blends each new fix with a prediction from the previous one, weighted by how much each is trusted) and an RTS smoother (a second, backward pass over a finished trip so that every point also learns from the fixes after it) both assume that each fix's error is a fresh coin toss. Phone errors are nothing of the kind. A 2025 field test against a centimetre-grade RTK reference split each phone's error into a constant offset of [-6.37, 1.00] m on a Motorola edge 40 neo and [-1.91, 1.99] m on a moto g24, plus a slowly wandering remainder whose errors stayed strongly correlated from one second to the next ([Kanzaki, Suto & Sato, arXiv 2609.11142](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.11142)). From that paper's fitted coefficients I calculate correlation times of 26 to 66 seconds. Pedestrian traces in Manhattan gave the same picture, with errors close to a random walk and with tall buildings mattering more than phone model or time of day ([Rutgers VTC paper](https://www.winlab.rutgers.edu/~gruteser/papers/VTC_camera_ready.pdf)). A study of garden visitors found residuals correlated out to 2 minutes, changing gradually instead of at random ([arXiv 2107.03925](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2107.03925)). None of these is an iPhone, and no correlation time for CoreLocation output has been published. Apple's output is fused with Wi-Fi and motion data before delivery, so its correlation is more likely longer than shorter, which is reasoned and unmeasured.

In the same paper, a smoother that modelled the offset and the exact wandering remainder still did about as well as no correction at all, because a track's shape cannot tell "the phone is 6 m off" apart from "the person walked 6 m to the side" ([arXiv 2609.11142](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.11142)). Only outside information removed the offset. So no filter tuning in LifePath, however clever, can take out the per-trip offset; that takes a map or another sensor.

LifePath's synthetic test adds independent Gaussian noise per fix, reports hAcc as 1.5 times the per-axis sigma (sigma meaning standard deviation, the typical size of an error), injects multipath bursts (fixes thrown off by signals bouncing off buildings), and requires a median of at most 8 m live, 6 m smoothed and no corner cut above 10 m (LifePath code, `Core/Tests/CoreTests/Synthetic.swift`). The hAcc convention holds up, because Apple defines horizontalAccuracy as a two-dimensional root-mean-square error, about 1.41 times the per-axis sigma for round errors, with the truth inside the circle 63 to 68% of the time ([Apple engineer, forum thread 699996](https://developer.apple.com/forums/thread/699996)). The 1.5 factor puts 67.5% inside, which is my calculation and a slightly cautious choice. At walking pace with a 10 m distance filter, fixes arrive about every 7 seconds. With a 45-second correlation time, neighbouring errors still correlate at about 0.86, so averaging N fixes gives roughly N/13 independent samples instead of N (my calculation). The smoother then believes itself about 3 to 4 times more certain than it is. At stops, where LifePath drops the distance filter and fixes come every second, the overconfidence is far worse. A test that hands the smoother independent noise grades it on exactly the averaging that drifting errors defeat ([Ranacher et al., arXiv 1504.04504](https://arxiv.org/abs/1504.04504) shows the same mechanism inflating recorded distances).

The change sits in the test file only. Each axis gets a slowly drifting error (a first-order Gauss–Markov process, meaning random error that wanders and forgets its past over a time constant) with a time constant of 30 to 60 seconds on foot, a constant per-trip offset of 0 to 6 m, a smaller white part, and occasional bounce episodes of 10 to 50 m lasting 5 to 60 seconds with a heavy-tailed size. Student-t fits to urban receiver errors in Hong Kong gave 2.5 to 4.7 degrees of freedom ([Li, Yan, Song & Hsu, arXiv 2603.16420](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.16420)), and an iPhone 6 at surveyed points produced one fix 99.7 m off among about 950 ([Merry & Bettinger 2019, PLOS ONE](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0219890)). The reported hAcc should follow the true error only loosely and run too small during bounce episodes, since an independent study found that phones' own accuracy estimates tracked the observed error poorly ([Zandbergen & Barbeau 2011](https://cambridge.org/core/journals/journal-of-navigation/article/positional-accuracy-of-assisted-gps-data-from-highsensitivity-gpsenabled-mobile-phones/E1EE20CD1A301C537BEE8EC66766B0A9)). Run about 20 fixed seeds per scenario so one lucky seed cannot pass a bad filter. Expect the medians to rise past the current bars; the right response is to retune the bars to the new noise and leave the filter alone until something is shown to beat them. Battery and warmth do not change, since nothing runs on the phone. The time constant is an assumption until LifePath measures its own, which the next section makes possible.

### Six numbers the health report can compute without ground truth

LifePath's health report already logs per-hour median hAcc and drop counts (LifePath code, `Core/Sources/Core/HealthReport.swift`). It can add six summary numbers that need no reference device. The filter's normalised innovation squared (a check of whether the filter's surprises match the uncertainty it claims; for two dimensions the average should sit near 2) shows overconfidence directly. Stop scatter compares the radius holding 68% of fixes during a stay with the median hAcc in that window; if well over 37% of fixes fall outside hAcc, the phone's own accuracy claim is too optimistic there. The path length ratio (raw, live and smoothed length divided by matched route length) catches zig-zag above 1 and corner cutting below 1. The spread of perpendicular offsets to the matched street, on confident straight stretches, shows whether smoothing tightens the line without shifting it. Repeat-route spread across many traversals of the same commute maps where buildings bend the signal. The sixth is the correlation time itself, read from the autocorrelation of cross-track residuals on straight matched streets. A combined indicator built from reference-free measures of this kind lined up with ground-truth measures on smartphone walking and cycling data ([Berjisian et al. 2023, IET ITS](https://ietresearch.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1049/itr2.12250)); the individual metrics here are my proposals. All six are numbers without coordinates, so they can go into the public repo or an issue without exposing where the owner lives, which matters because removing names does not anonymise a GPS trace ([Privacy risk in GeoData survey, arXiv 2402.03612](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.03612v2)). The fitted time constant, standard deviation and bounce rate can then replace the assumed values in the synthetic generator, so the test inherits the owner's error behaviour without any of the owner's places.

Free ground truth exists too. Walking deliberately along one side of a street whose sidewalk is mapped as its own OSM way, or along a straight canal edge, and standing still for 2 minutes at a recognisable corner gives a reference good to about 1 to 3 m, by my estimate. That is enough to check errors of 5 m and more and the 10 m corner bar. No published ground-truth test of the iPhone 15 Pro in a city or under trees was found, so these walks would produce the first. Tests of the earlier iPhone 14 Pro's dual-frequency GPS showed only about 1 to 2 m of gain in city walking ([AppleVis](https://applevis.com/comment/140158)).

## Two numbers the phone already sends get thrown away

Every CoreLocation fix carries `speedAccuracy` in metres per second and `courseAccuracy` in degrees, with negative values meaning invalid ([Apple, speedAccuracy](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/corelocation/cllocation/speedaccuracy.md); [Apple, courseAccuracy](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/corelocation/cllocation/courseaccuracy.md)). LifePath's `sample(_:)` keeps timestamp, position, hAcc, speed and course and drops both accuracy fields, while the filter and smoother use a fixed Doppler sigma of 0.7 m/s and fixed heading errors of 3° and 10° (LifePath code, `LifePath/Recorder/LocationService.swift`; `Core/Sources/Core/TrackFilter.swift`; `Core/Sources/Core/TripSmoother.swift`). Doppler speed is speed measured from the frequency shift of the satellite signals, and its errors are largely independent of the position errors that buildings cause, which is why it is the measurement best placed to pin down a track's shape between drifting fixes. On Android phones, processing raw Doppler improved position by about 37% east and north ([Peng et al., J. Navigation 2023](https://cambridge.org/core/journals/journal-of-navigation/article/characterisation-and-positionaiding-performance-analysis-of-doppler-observation-from-android-smartphones/B5163AAD5F99C885E4ADB5F27DD93B75)). That figure comes from raw satellite measurements with an unfiltered baseline; iOS speed and course are already smoothed by Apple, so the remaining gain for LifePath is much smaller and has never been measured.

At walking pace a phone's course is close to meaningless, yet a fixed 10° tells the filter to trust it. On a bike or in a vehicle, Apple can re-bind a fix to another road, and a 2015 test logged one-second speed spikes such as 87 km/h on a bicycle ([Jeffrey Friedl](https://regex.info/blog/2015-12-03/2651)); that report is old, but a fixed 0.7 m/s cannot down-weight such a spike while a per-fix accuracy can. The change is small. Add the two fields to the stored sample as optional values so old recordings load unchanged and fall back to the current constants. Use speed trust as max(speedAccuracy, 0.3 to 0.5 m/s) and sideways trust as speed times courseAccuracy in radians, with courseAccuracy floored at 3°, and fall back to 0.7 m/s and 3°/10° whenever a value is negative. Apple's initializer documentation swaps the descriptions of the two accuracy parameters, so the property pages are the ones to trust ([Apple CLLocation init](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/corelocation/cllocation/init(coordinate:altitude:horizontalaccuracy:verticalaccuracy:course:courseaccuracy:speed:speedaccuracy:timestamp:).md)). Nobody has published how well calibrated these two values are on any iPhone, so log them for two weeks and compare them with the smoothed velocity on clean stretches before the filter relies on them. Battery and warmth do not change.

The same check settles a smaller question about hAcc. LifePath uses hAcc as the per-axis sigma, so its measurement variance is hAcc squared (LifePath code, `TrackFilter.swift`). Apple's definition makes the per-axis sigma about hAcc divided by 1.41, so the filter trusts each fix half as much as Apple's ideal numbers suggest ([forum thread 699996](https://developer.apple.com/forums/thread/699996)). Given the drifting errors above, that accidental doubling helps, and "correcting" it on its own would make the filter more overconfident. Leave the value and add a one-line comment saying the inflation stands in for correlated error, until the drift states in the next section make it explicit.

One more guard costs one line. Apple's older location guide warns that the first events after starting can be cached and drops events older than 15 seconds in its sample code ([Location and Maps Programming Guide](https://developer.apple.com/library/archive/documentation/UserExperience/Conceptual/LocationAwarenessPG/CoreLocation/CoreLocation.html)). LifePath's live filter accepts fixes up to 10 seconds old (LifePath code, `TrackFilter.swift`), which is stricter, but a fix stamped before the GPS was switched on should never start a trip. If the code does not already reject those, it should.

## The smoother can model drift it cannot remove

Two extra numbers in the smoother's state, one drift per axis wandering with the measured time constant, will not shrink the per-trip offset; the Kanzaki result rules that out. They do three useful things, all reasoned rather than measured on phone data. The smoother's covariance (its own belief about how uncertain it is) starts to match its actual error, so LifePath's robust gating rejects the fixes it should and keeps the ones it should. Slow drift stops being read as movement, which is what draws little false wiggles and creeping lines at stops. And Doppler velocity, whose errors are independent, gets to separate drift from actual motion. In a simulation of a different system (camera-aided inertial navigation), a filter that modelled correlated noise correctly beat a mis-modelled one by 13.8 to 16.1% on average error and reported uncertainties that matched its errors ([ISPRS Annals II-2, 2014](https://isprs-annals.copernicus.org/articles/II-2/61/2014/isprsannals-II-2-61-2014.pdf)). That transfers weakly at best. For LifePath I expect the median to move by single-digit percentages and the gain to show up in the line's shape and in fewer false metres. The work belongs in `TripSmoother.swift` first, since it runs only while the app is open or charging, which suits the rule that heavy work waits for those moments. A filter with two more states per step costs microseconds, by my estimate, so the live filter could follow later if the offline version beats the new test bars.

At stops, the motion chip can supply a measurement of its own. When CoreMotion says stationary and the pedometer shows no steps, the smoother can take "speed is zero, give or take 0.1 m/s" as a measurement. Phone-in-pocket versions of the foot-mounted zero-velocity trick are unreliable ([arXiv 1703.00154](https://arxiv.org/html/1703.00154)), but whole-stop detection from activity plus step count is a different and sounder signal, and LifePath's motion engine already computes it (LifePath code, `Core/Sources/Core/MotionEngine.swift`). Averaging during stops is one of the few position-only steps that carried over from the Google Smartphone Decimeter Challenge, a competition on phone positioning, where an 18th-place pipeline's Kalman step moved its score only from 5.29 to 5.21 ([Speakerdeck, 18th place GSDC](https://speakerdeck.com/kuto5046/18th-place-solution-in-gsdc)). The payoff for a diary is the daily distance, because noise around a stop piles up as metres never walked, and drifting noise inflates recorded distance in a way that depends on how strongly it is correlated ([Ranacher et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/1504.04504)). A related, equally cheap step is to use CMPedometer's distance between two consecutive fixes as a constraint on path length, instead of only the 30-second pace LifePath already feeds in; it is unmeasured and small. Neither change touches battery, since CoreMotion is already running.

The largest measured gain in the phone literature, in contrast, comes from full pedestrian dead reckoning (estimating movement from step counts plus compass heading). Adding it cut mean error by 24.4% on a campus and 19.4% in a dense Hong Kong street canyon ([Wen/Hsu group, arXiv 2212.14264](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2212.14264)). It needs continuous device-motion heading in the background, whose battery cost on iPhone has not been published, a phone in a pocket confuses the heading, and the study used raw satellite measurements iOS does not expose. It fails the battery and warmth goals until someone measures otherwise.

## Apple bends the track before LifePath sees it

LifePath records on foot and by bike with activity type `.fitness`, and with `.otherNavigation` when CoreMotion says automotive (LifePath code, `LifePath/Recorder/LocationService.swift`). Apple's developer support has stated that the activity type controls how CoreLocation processes raw locations before the app receives them, including correction toward known roads for navigation and for fitness ([Apple DTS, forum thread 768991](https://developer.apple.com/forums/thread/768991)). The same engineer wrote that `.fitness` delivers corrected coordinates, that `.other` stops following known paths but also loses the jitter reduction, and that when one app runs two managers, the settings of one override the other, leaning toward the more demanding settings ([Apple DTS, forum thread 765927](https://developer.apple.com/forums/thread/765927)). In August 2026, testing on iOS 26.6.1, Apple added that "road binding is tied to activity type" ([forum thread 843376](https://developer.apple.com/forums/thread/843376)). Developers report the opposite in places: `.other` still snapping outdoors, and 4x4 tracks snapping to roads with both `.other` and `.otherNavigation` ([thread 765927](https://developer.apple.com/forums/thread/765927); [thread 768991](https://developer.apple.com/forums/thread/768991)). A boat whose fixes jumped to the shore with both types matches an Apple engineer's explanation that the system was correcting to a plausible spot on the shore, which bears on the waterbus ([thread 797906](https://developer.apple.com/forums/thread/797906)).

Two things follow for LifePath. Its walks are probably being map-matched by Apple before its own filter and map matcher see them, so a walk across a square can arrive already pulled toward a path, and LifePath's honesty rule cannot catch what it never sees. Whether `.other` would serve accuracy better on foot is unknowable from the sources, since Apple says it adds jitter and developers say it does not stop snapping. That calls for a one-week experiment on the known-path walks above, comparing perpendicular-offset spread and length ratio between the two types, using a temporary build flag instead of a new user setting. The second point is cheaper. The 999 m keep-alive manager runs alongside the recording manager with the default activity type (LifePath code, `LocationService.swift`), and Apple says the two managers' settings merge toward the more demanding one, which leaves unclear which processing LifePath's fixes get. Setting the keep-alive's activity type to the recording type inside the existing `configure` call is one line, makes both managers ask for the same treatment, and costs nothing. Its gain is unknown, and the experiment should run after it so the comparison is clean.

The distance filter deserves a look because it discards data. Apple documents it as a gate measured from the last delivered fix ([Apple, distanceFilter](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/corelocation/cllocationmanager/distancefilter.md)), so at walking pace a 10 m filter drops about six of every seven fixes the GPS produces (my calculation). With errors correlated at about 0.86 between fixes 7 seconds apart, those dropped positions carry little new information; what they carry is Doppler speed. Turning the filter off on foot would wake the app seven times as often for a gain nobody has measured, while the battery research for this project found that per-fix wake-ups, more than the GPS chip, drive drain ([Apple forum 844579](https://developer.apple.com/forums/thread/844579)). Keep 10 m. In vehicles the filter already passes a fix every second above 36 km/h, so there is nothing to gain there.

BestForNavigation stays where it is. Apple describes it as drawing on extra sensor data and reserves it for a plugged-in phone ([Apple, kCLLocationAccuracyBestForNavigation](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/corelocation/kcllocationaccuracybestfornavigation.md)), and the one published comparison found no significant accuracy difference from Best ([Flybuy](https://www.flybuy.com/?p=2471)). LifePath's choice of Best at Balanced and BestForNavigation only at Max fits both. The 65 m rough-fix rule also matches an Apple engineer's statement that without a satellite fix CoreLocation falls back to Wi-Fi, whose fixes report 65 m or more ([Apple engineer, thread 797906](https://developer.apple.com/forums/thread/797906)). On iOS 26 some underground networks get positions produced without satellites, with Apple confirming work with New York's MTA ([thread 802721](https://developer.apple.com/forums/thread/802721)), so tunnel fixes can look plausible while being modelled, which LifePath's transit snapping already treats with suspicion.

The owner can help for free. Location Services uses cellular, Wi-Fi, GPS and Bluetooth, and Apple's crowd-sourced hotspot database fills in where satellites are weak ([Apple support 102515](https://support.apple.com/en-us/102515)), so Wi-Fi switched on (connected or not) and Bluetooth on both help. Precise Location must stay on for LifePath, since BestForNavigation requires it. Apple's Low Power Mode page does not list location among the things it reduces ([Apple support 101604](https://support.apple.com/101604)).

## Map matching was tuned on cars sampled every second

LifePath's map matcher follows Newson and Krumm's hidden Markov model (a method that picks which street each fix belongs to by scoring both its distance to the street and how sensible the route between consecutive picks is). It uses sigma = max(4 m, hAcc), a constant beta of 3 m (50 m for rough fixes), thins fixes within 2 sigma of the last used one, and has no off-network state (LifePath code, `Core/Sources/Core/MapMatcher.swift`). Beta sets how much detour between two fixes the matcher tolerates. LifePath's sigma and beta match Valhalla's defaults (sigma 4.07, beta 3) ([Valhalla Meili configuration](https://valhalla.github.io/valhalla/contributing/architecture/meili/configuration/)), GraphHopper uses 10 m and 2 ([GraphHopper MapMatching.java](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/graphhopper/graphhopper/master/map-matching/src/main/java/com/graphhopper/matching/MapMatching.java)), and all of these values come from car traces. The best beta depends on measurement noise and on the spacing between samples ([Multi-track Map Matching, arXiv 1209.2759](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1209.2759)). For a walker sampled every 7 to 40 m, a fixed 3 m punishes every crossing and path bend, so I propose beta at about 0.1 to 0.2 times the straight-line step, never below 3 m. The rough-fix value of 50 m already leans this way. The per-fix sigma is stricter and better motivated than any engine's single value and can stay.

For honesty, the missing off-network state weighs more than beta. OSM sidewalks are accurate where they exist, but one study found them missing at 72% of points where Street View showed a sidewalk ([CDC Preventing Chronic Disease 2026](https://www.cdc.gov/pcd/issues/2026/26_0255.htm)), and squares and parks often have no ways at all. Pedestrian matching papers report gains from handling these "open-field" breaks explicitly ([Enhanced HMM for pedestrian map matching](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/380145567_An_Enhanced_Hidden_Markov_Model_for_Map-Matching_in_Pedestrian_Navigation)), with claimed, dataset-specific figures. The usual remedy is one extra candidate per fix, the fix itself, scored from hAcc with a fixed penalty, so that when no street explains a fix better, the line stays where it was measured instead of being dragged to the nearest edge. A U-turn penalty in the style of GraphHopper's (40) stops a walk from zig-zagging between parallel footways. A heading term from Doppler course helps only above walking speed and when courseAccuracy is small, and no source isolates a measured gain from one, so it ranks last for walkers. If daily distance is ever measured along the matched line, GraphHopper's habit of carrying thinned fixes' distance into the next step keeps the total exact.

None of the published defaults were tuned on pedestrians, so the only trustworthy setting comes from a small sweep of beta and the off-network penalty on the owner's known-path walks, scored with the offset spread and length ratio from the health report. The code change is small, it runs offline, and the live battery cost is zero.

## The walk out the front door can be drawn as a marked guess

Each trip starts at its first GPS fix, with the departure time dated precisely from CoreMotion; at home, the GPS switches on after a keep-alive reading outside the place, the 200 m+ geofence, or 3 minutes of walking pace, and a design note accepts that "the first tens of metres after leaving home can stay a gap" (LifePath code, `Core/Sources/Core/Trips.swift`, `Core/Sources/Core/MotionEngine.swift`, `docs/decisions/v0.3.1.md`). Shrinking the geofence would not help, since developer reports put iOS exit latency anywhere from about 20 seconds to 3 to 5 minutes, and regions of 50 m or less are the most common cause of trouble ([Apple forum 768373](https://developer.apple.com/forums/thread/768373); [Capawesome](https://capawesome.io/blog/why-are-your-geofences-not-triggering-on-ios/)). The pedometer wake already reacts within steps, so most of the gap is the time the GPS needs for a good first fix, which nobody has measured on an iPhone 15 Pro.

Arc's open-source engine, LocoKit2, draws no route through gaps. It links neighbouring items directly when the gap is 15 minutes or less, inserts an explicit data-gap item when longer, and fixes trip starts only by moving boundary samples between visit and trip ([LocoKit2 EdgeHealing](https://github.com/sobri909/LocoKit2/blob/main/Sources/LocoKit2/Managers/TimelineProcessor+EdgeHealing.swift); [LocoKit2 EdgeCleansing](https://github.com/sobri909/LocoKit2/blob/main/Sources/LocoKit2/Managers/TimelineProcessor+EdgeCleansing.swift)). Its boundary rule is worth borrowing as is. A moving sample counts as still at the place only within the mean plus one standard deviation of the visit's radius, so a poor edge fix cannot drag the visit.

LifePath's trip-start gap is better posed than the route-inference problems in the literature, because both ends, the elapsed time and the walked distance are known. The proposal is to find the few shortest foot paths on OSM from the place's exit to the first fix, choose the one whose length best matches CMPedometer's distance over the gap, and reject any path implying more than walking pace or more than five times the straight distance, Valhalla's own routing limit ([Valhalla Meili configuration](https://valhalla.github.io/valhalla/contributing/architecture/meili/configuration/)). No published work uses step distance to choose among outdoor paths, so this is untested; for short gaps the shortest path will usually win anyway. The honesty rule decides how it appears. It must be stored as inferred, drawn dashed or faded, and kept out of recorded kilometres, either left uncounted, as the project already does for signal gaps, or shown as a separate "inferred" figure. Route-inference research makes the same point, warning that the single most likely route can be wrong under high uncertainty ([InferTra, arXiv 1603.07641](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1603.07641)). The cost is moderate, since LifePath needs a foot router on its OSM data and a second line style. It changes completeness, not accuracy, so it ranks below the changes that improve recorded track.

## Ideas that fail the goals

| Idea | Why it fails |
|---|---|
| BestForNavigation all day | Apple ties it to a plugged-in phone and the one comparison found no accuracy gain ([Flybuy](https://www.flybuy.com/?p=2471)). |
| IMM smoother, Student-t variational smoother, factor graphs | An IMM smoother (several motion models weighed against each other) performed about like a single constant-velocity smoother in a survey ([arXiv 1503.07828](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1503.07828)); the robust alternatives cost 5 to 10 times a Kalman filter and are proven only on raw satellite data ([arXiv 1503.06606](https://web3.arxiv.org/pdf/1503.06606)). |
| Full step-and-compass dead reckoning in the background | Largest measured gain, but on raw data, with unknown background battery cost and pocket heading confusion. |
| Shadow matching with 3D building models | The 11.25 to 2.41 m result needs per-satellite signal data iOS never exposes ([UCL](https://discovery-pp.ucl.ac.uk/1421172/1/GNSS14-0181.pdf)). |
| Fixes every second on foot | Seven times the wake-ups for positions that are about 86% correlated with their neighbours. |
| A u-blox ZED-F9P RTK receiver as ground truth | The chip alone is under 200 USD ([Hohensinn et al. 2022](https://repositum.tuwien.at/bitstream/20.500.12708/142091/1/Hohensinn-2022-Remote%20Sensing-vor.pdf)); a working board and antenna breaks the €0 rule. |
| Public datasets (GSDC, UrbanNav) as an iPhone benchmark | They hold Android phones on car roofs, with no iPhone and no walking ([Hsu et al., NAVI](https://navi.ion.org/content/70/4/navi.602)). |
| GSDC "snap to grid" | Its grids were built from ground truth, which LifePath never has ([Speakerdeck](https://speakerdeck.com/kuto5046/18th-place-solution-in-gsdc)). |
| Geofence below 100 m | Small regions are the most common cause of missed exits. |
| Forcing walks onto the nearest way | Breaks in parks and squares and draws paths that were never walked. |
| Committing recorded traces to the public repo | GPS traces identify a person even without names; commit only fitted parameters. |

## Conclusion

The research moves the accuracy question away from filter design. On the input iOS gives an app, a smoother cannot see the per-trip offset of a few metres, and the measured gains from fancier smoothers are close to nil, so on the evidence available LifePath's filter has little left to win from smoothing alone. What remains splits into three kinds of work. The first is measuring against realistic errors, so a change that helps is visible and one that only helps on paper is not. The second is feeding the filter information it currently drops or ignores: per-fix accuracy fields, stops, and pedometer distance. The third is outside knowledge, meaning the map and the motion chip, which is the only lever against the offset and the reason the building-footprint prior keeps a place on the list despite being unproven. Every item in the top seven costs nothing in battery or warmth, so none of them forces a trade among the three top priorities.

Almost every number about iPhone behaviour in this report is missing from the literature: no correlation time for CoreLocation output, no calibration of its accuracy fields, no 15 Pro walking test, no measured effect of `.fitness` road binding. The owner's own phone, the health report and a few deliberate walks along mapped sidewalks can produce all of them for €0, and those measurements should set the order of the remaining work instead of this ranking.
